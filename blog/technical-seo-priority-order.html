Technical SEO in Priority Order: What to Fix First

Technical SEO advice almost always arrives as a flat checklist of two hundred items. It isn't flat. A handful of things are binary and decide whether you exist in search at all; most of the rest are refinements. Here's the work in the order I'd actually do it.

Published
11 November 2026
Reading time
10 minutes
Written by
Bhagyashri Chudji, full-stack developer, Mumbai

Why the flat checklist is the problem

Run any automated SEO tool over a site and you'll get a long, undifferentiated list: missing alt text, a redirect chain, an image that could be 40KB smaller, no structured data, a heading level skipped. Every item looks equally like a job.

They are not equal. A stray noindex tag removes you from search entirely. A missing alt attribute costs you very close to nothing in ranking terms. Those two sit next to each other in the export, scored the same shade of amber, and that's how businesses end up paying for a month of small refinements while the thing actually suppressing them goes unnoticed.

So the useful question isn't "what's wrong with my site." It's "what's wrong with my site in the order that matters." The levels below are roughly that order. Each one is close to pointless until the one above it is satisfied.

Level 0: can a crawler reach the page at all?

This is binary. If the answer is no, nothing else on this page matters, and no amount of content or speed work will change anything.

The usual culprits are small and specific: a robots.txt rule left too broad after launch, a noindex meta tag carried over from a staging environment, pages sitting behind a login or a geo-restriction, or a server returning errors to crawlers while serving humans normally.

The staging noindex is the classic. A site is built on a staging URL with indexing deliberately blocked, then pushed live with the block still in place. Everything looks perfect to the owner. The site is simply invisible, sometimes for months, and the cause is one line of HTML.

A trap worth naming: the site: search

Before you conclude you have a Level 0 problem, be careful how you check, because the standard method is unreliable and I have a recent example from my own site.

An SEO audit of this site concluded, as its single highest-priority finding, that the site was not indexed at all. The evidence was a site: search in Google that came back empty. That finding sat at the top of the document as the blocker everything else depended on.

It was wrong. Search Console showed the homepage indexed and crawling normally. The site: operator is a rough diagnostic, not a source of truth: it's known to return incomplete or empty results, and it's least reliable exactly where people reach for it most, on new domains with few pages. The entire emergency was an artefact of the measurement.

The lesson generalises beyond that one audit. Check indexing in Google Search Console, not with a site: search. Search Console tells you what Google has actually done with each URL, why a page was excluded, and when it was last crawled. Any audit that declares a site unindexed without opening Search Console has not established the thing it's claiming, and if that claim is at the top of a document you're paying to act on, it's worth verifying before you spend anything.

Level 1: is it indexed, and does Search Console agree?

Set up Search Console if you haven't. It's free, verification takes minutes, and it's the only view of how search engines actually treat your site.

The report to read is Indexing, then Pages. It splits your URLs into indexed and not indexed, and for the second group it gives you a reason for each one. Those reasons are specific and actionable: discovered but not crawled, crawled but not indexed, excluded by a noindex, an alternate page with a canonical pointing elsewhere, a redirect, a soft 404.

Submit a sitemap while you're there, and confirm it lists the URLs you actually want indexed and nothing else. A sitemap full of redirects, dead pages, or URLs blocked in robots.txt sends a confused signal about what matters on your site.

One realistic expectation to set: being indexed is not the same as ranking. Indexing means you are eligible to appear. On a new site, the gap between the two is normal and measured in months, and no technical fix compresses it.

Level 2: is the right page the one being indexed?

Once pages are being indexed, the next failure mode is that the wrong version of them is, or that several versions compete with each other.

  • Canonical tags. Every page should declare its own canonical URL. When the same content is reachable at several addresses, with and without a trailing slash, with tracking parameters attached, on both a www and a bare domain, the canonical tells search engines which one counts.
  • One home for each piece of content. Pick HTTPS, pick www or no-www, and redirect everything else to it once, with a 301.
  • Redirect chains. One hop is fine. Four hops in sequence waste crawl budget and lose value at each step.
  • Near-duplicate pages. Several thin pages targeting almost the same thing compete with each other rather than adding up. One good page usually outperforms four weak ones.

This is also where a redesign does its quietest damage, because URLs change and nothing maps the old ones to the new. I've written separately about what to check before committing to a redesign, and redirect mapping is the single item on that list I'd never let be dropped from a scope.

Level 3: can a crawler read the content once it arrives?

A page can return a perfectly healthy response and still be effectively empty to a crawler. This happens on front-ends that render everything in the browser with JavaScript: the HTML that arrives first is a near-blank shell, and the content only appears after scripts run.

Google does render JavaScript, but rendering is a second pass, it's slower, and it is not guaranteed to be complete. Other crawlers, including several that feed AI assistants, are considerably less patient. The result is a site that looks fine to you and thin to everything that matters.

To check, view the page source rather than the inspector, or fetch the URL with JavaScript disabled, and see whether your actual headings and body copy are present. If they aren't, the fix is server-side rendering or pre-rendering, which is backend work rather than a content or marketing task, and it's worth knowing that before you brief someone.

Level 4: speed, and what it really buys you

Performance matters, and it matters less as a ranking lever than it does as a reason people leave. Treat it as both.

Measure with PageSpeed Insights on the mobile tab, and look at the Core Web Vitals: how long the main content takes to appear, how fast the page responds to a tap, and how much the layout shifts while loading. Google publishes thresholds for each and adjusts them periodically, so read the current numbers from the tool rather than from an article like this one.

The causes are usually unglamorous and fixable: images served far larger than they're displayed, render-blocking scripts in the head, fonts loading late and shifting text, and a framework doing work the page never needed. Fixing those helps your visitors immediately, which is the honest reason to do it. Any ranking benefit is a tiebreaker between otherwise comparable pages, not a way to leapfrog better content.

Level 5: on-page fundamentals

Cheap, fast, genuinely worth doing, and nowhere near as decisive as the levels above:

  • A unique, descriptive title tag on every page. Duplicates across a site are common and easy to fix.
  • A meta description written for a human deciding whether to click. It isn't a ranking factor, it is a click-through factor.
  • One h1 per page, with a heading structure that follows the actual outline.
  • Descriptive alt text on images that carry meaning, empty alt on decorative ones.
  • Internal links that use real anchor text and point at the pages you want to perform.

That last point is underrated and free. If a page is four clicks from anywhere and nothing links to it with meaningful text, you've told search engines it isn't important. Fixing internal linking costs nothing but attention.

Level 6: structured data

JSON-LD schema does not make you rank. What it does is describe your pages in a machine-readable form, which makes you eligible for richer result formats and increasingly shapes how AI-powered search and assistants summarise you.

Keep it truthful and keep it matched to what's visibly on the page. Marking up reviews you don't have, or an FAQ that doesn't appear to visitors, is the kind of thing that attracts a manual penalty rather than a rich result. Describe what's genuinely there and nothing else.

What technical SEO cannot do

Worth stating plainly, because this is where expectations usually break:

  • It cannot make thin or unhelpful content rank. It removes obstacles; it doesn't manufacture merit.
  • It cannot guarantee a position. Nobody can. Anyone selling you a ranking guarantee is either misinformed or misleading you.
  • It cannot produce fast results in competitive markets. Fixes land immediately; recrawling and reassessment take weeks, and competitive positions take months.
  • It cannot substitute for having something worth finding.

The honest framing is that technical SEO stops your site being held back by problems that are entirely within your control. That's a real and often large effect, and it is a different claim from "we'll get you to the top of Google."

The order I'd work in

Confirm crawlability and indexing in Search Console first, and fix anything blocking either. Then make sure the correct single version of each page is the one indexed. Then check that a crawler can actually read the content. Then measure speed on mobile and fix the specific causes. Then tidy titles, headings, and internal links. Then add structured data that reflects reality.

Most sites I look at have a problem in the first three levels and a list of Level 5 suggestions from an automated tool. Working top down means the first thing you fix is usually the thing that was actually costing you something, and you'll know within a few weeks in Search Console whether it moved.

The short version

Check indexing in Search Console, never with a site: search. Fix anything that blocks crawling or indexing before touching anything else. Make sure one canonical version of each page is the one being indexed, and that a crawler can read its content without running JavaScript. Then speed, then on-page basics, then schema.

Done in that order, the first fix is usually the one that mattered. Done as a flat checklist, you can spend a month on alt text while a single stray tag keeps you out of search entirely.
